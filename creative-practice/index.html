Creative Practice

Ceramics, sketching, and visual design are another part of how I learn to make things — through observation, experimentation, failure, and refinement.

Making as a way of thinking

My interests in ceramics, sketching, and visual design strengthen many of the same iterative skills I use in engineering: observation, prototyping, material exploration, and refinement through critique.

Working creatively has taught me to pay attention not only to whether something functions, but also to how it feels, how it is used, and how decisions about form and material shape the final experience.

Good ideas become better through making, testing, seeing what fails, and trying again.

Creative practice → engineering practice

The medium changes, but the process is surprisingly similar. Both creative and engineering work require translating an idea into something physical, evaluating what actually happens, and being willing to revise it.

01

Observe

Pay attention to form, behavior, context, and the details that are easy to overlook.

02

Prototype

Move ideas out of your head and into a form that can actually be evaluated.

03

Test

Let materials, users, and real constraints expose assumptions that were invisible on paper.

04

Refine

Use critique and failure as information, then make the next iteration more intentional.

What making has taught me

I’ve learned a lot from designs that looked good on paper but failed under real constraints. Those moments have made me a better builder because they force me to question assumptions, understand materials more deeply, and treat iteration as part of the process rather than as a correction at the end.

I also value community, service, and making things that feel tangible and useful. Whether I’m working on an engineering prototype or something made by hand, I’m most interested in work that connects thoughtful design with real people and real experiences.
